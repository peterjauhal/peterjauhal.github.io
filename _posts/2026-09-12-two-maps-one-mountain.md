I've sat with this question longer than I can comfortably admit. Advaita Vedanta and Tibetan Buddhism look, from the outside, like two traditions in disagreement. One says there is a self — the true self, the *atman* — identical with Brahman. The other says there is no self at all, *anatman*, and that the recognition of this absence is the very door to freedom.

They can't both be right, or so it appears.

Then I remembered something I've noticed on my own cushion. The doctrine is where they part. The experience is where they converge.

Let me try to say it without the usual mush. In Advaita, the method is recognition. You are not to become awareness. You already are awareness. The work is to stop mistaking the seer for what is seen — *neti, neti*, not this, not this — until only that which sees is left. The arrival is not something new arriving. It is what was standing here all along, labelling itself "me."

In Mahamudra, and in the dzogchen that sits above it, the language is emptiness and clarity — *shunyata* and the luminous knowing that knows. You are not to build a self, and you are not to fight one off. You rest in the natural state, the ground that is empty of independent existence and vivid in awareness. The arrival reads the same: an object that is not separate from what sees it, but the seeing itself.

From the outside, these read as opposites. One affirms. One negates. But on the cushion, I think they arrive in the same room. The self you recognise, and the selflessness you rest in, are two names for one ground, approached from two directions.

That is why I hold the difference matters less than the communities claim. I've watched earnest people spend years arguing whether the ultimate is Brahman or emptiness while quietly treating the map as the mountain. A map with a label is still a map.

Where the two genuinely part is not the goal, but the terrain they describe. Brahman is a ground — substantial, the source the world pours out of. Emptiness is the opposite: no ground, no independent existence anywhere, and it is precisely this absence that is free. That is a real philosophical fork, and I won't wave it away. But it is a fork in the description, not in the practice.

For the person who sits daily — and for me that is the only question that carries any weight — what does the difference change? The breath. The posture. The moment the mind starts doing theology instead of resting. Both traditions arrive at the same practical instruction: do not make the seer into a thing.

So here is my read, said with the humility of someone still on the mountain rather than at the top. The disagreement is real and worth honouring. But for the practice, the recognition and the rest are closer than either side admits, and the instruction they leave on the shelf is the same. Two maps. One mountain. The summit doesn't care which map you bring.
